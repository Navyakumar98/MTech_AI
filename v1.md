# JobFusion: An AI-Driven Smart Candidate Ranking and Resume Screening System

---

## Abstract

Recruitment has become one of the most demanding processes in modern organizations because the volume of applications has grown rapidly while the time available for shortlisting has reduced. Traditional resume screening, which depends on manual reading and keyword matching, often becomes inconsistent and time-consuming when recruiters have to evaluate hundreds of resumes for a single role [13], [14]. This thesis presents **JobFusion**, an AI-driven recruitment intelligence platform designed to address these limitations by combining semantic understanding of resumes and job descriptions with feedback-aware ranking and quantitative ranking quality measurement.

JobFusion is built as a two-sided platform. On the jobseeker side, users upload their resumes in PDF (Portable Document Format) form, receive a ranked list of suitable jobs, and refine the recommendations by submitting feedback in the form of ratings. On the recruiter side, users submit a Job Description (JD), retrieve a ranked list of candidates from a stored resume corpus, and improve the shortlist through recruiter ratings. The system is developed using React for the web frontend [15], Flask for the backend [16], Firebase Authentication with Firestore for user management [17], and a Python-based artificial intelligence layer that uses Sentence-BERT (SBERT) embeddings [1], TF-IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency) pre-filtering [6], and FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) for fast similarity search [3], [4], [5]. The web application is deployed and publicly accessible [20], and the platform is extended through companion Android and iOS applications that consume the same backend services.

The ranking layer of JobFusion follows a hybrid approach. It combines a semantic similarity score, a skill overlap score, an experience compatibility score, and additional preference factors such as location and salary fit [10], [11]. After candidates rate suggestions or recruiters rate shortlisted candidates, the system applies feedback-aware reranking to bring more preference-aligned results to the top [9]. The quality of ranking is measured using established metrics such as NDCG (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain), Spearman Rank Correlation, Precision@K, and the percentage of candidates whose positions changed after feedback was applied [7], [8]. These metrics together describe not just whether the top results are good, but also how stable and how personalized the ranking has become.

Results obtained from the prototype show that the baseline ranking is already strong because of semantic embeddings, and feedback-aware reranking adds further alignment with user expectations without disturbing the overall structure. The system produces clear match percentages, candidate lists, ranking comparisons before and after feedback, and exportable shortlists. Overall, JobFusion demonstrates that practical AI methods can be combined into a clean, interpretable, and usable recruitment platform that helps both jobseekers and recruiters make better, faster, and more informed decisions.

## Chapter 1 – Introduction

### 1.1 Introduction to Recruitment Systems

Recruitment is the process through which organizations identify, evaluate, and select candidates for open positions. Earlier, recruitment was largely a paper-based, manual activity in which Human Resources (HR) teams collected resumes, read them one by one, and prepared shortlists for interviews. As the internet matured, this activity moved to digital portals, email-based applications, and Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) that store, search, and filter resumes. Today, most large organizations rely on some form of automated screening because the number of applications received for a single opening can easily run into the hundreds or even thousands [14].

Even with this digitization, recruitment continues to face a fundamental difficulty: matching a person’s skills, experience, and background with what a role actually requires is not a simple comparison of words. Two candidates may use very different vocabulary to describe similar abilities, and two job descriptions may emphasize different aspects of the same role. The mismatch between how skills are described and how job requirements are written is one of the main reasons recruitment software still misses suitable candidates and presents unsuitable ones at the top of the list [13].

### 1.2 Need for AI in Hiring

Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly through Machine Learning (ML) and Natural Language Processing (NLP), has changed how text-based systems understand human language. Modern language models can read a sentence and convert it into a numerical representation called an embedding, where similar meanings are close together in mathematical space, even if the actual words used are different [1], [2]. Recruitment is a natural fit for this kind of technology because the central challenge is comparing meaning, not just text.

AI-based hiring tools can read a resume and understand the context of a candidate’s work history. They can compare it semantically against a job description, identify which skills are relevant, and assign a score that reflects how well the two match [6]. Beyond static matching, AI can learn from recruiter behavior. When recruiters consistently rate certain candidates highly, that information can be used to adjust future rankings [9], [10]. This combination of semantic matching and learning from feedback is what gives modern hiring systems an advantage over older keyword-based screeners.

### 1.3 Resume Screening Problems

Manual resume screening becomes unreliable when application volumes are high. A recruiter who spends only a few seconds on each resume can easily miss strong profiles because of small formatting issues, missing keywords, or unfamiliar terminology. Automated keyword filters reduce reading time but introduce new problems. They tend to reject candidates whose resumes use different but equivalent terms, they reward candidates who stuff keywords into their resumes, and they cannot judge the depth of experience behind a particular skill [13]. As a result, both kinds of screening, manual and keyword-driven, leave room for improvement.

There is also a second problem on the candidate side. Many qualified people apply to positions that are not the best match for their actual abilities because job portals show jobs based on location and category, not on a deep understanding of the person’s profile. Jobseekers waste time applying to mismatched roles, and recruiters waste time filtering through irrelevant applications. A two-sided system that helps both groups simultaneously addresses this inefficiency.

### 1.4 Motivation of the Project

The motivation behind JobFusion comes directly from the observation that existing systems handle each side of the hiring market separately and rarely incorporate feedback in a meaningful way. There are job portals for candidates and screening tools for recruiters, but most of them work like keyword search engines. Even when they include AI features, the explanations behind a match are usually hidden, and there is no easy way for the user to influence the next round of results [11], [12].

Another motivation is the importance of transparent evaluation. In a hiring context, the people involved need to understand why one candidate is ranked above another. A system that simply returns a list without a clear notion of how good the ranking is, or how much it changed after feedback, cannot be trusted in serious decision-making. JobFusion is designed to provide this transparency by computing standard ranking quality metrics and showing the user what changes happened after each round of feedback [7], [8].

### 1.5 Objectives of the Project

The objectives of this thesis can be stated naturally as follows. First, to build a unified web-based system that helps both jobseekers and recruiters through dedicated modules. Second, to integrate semantic matching using SBERT embeddings and FAISS-based similarity search so that candidates and jobs are compared by meaning, not just words [1], [3], [4]. Third, to add a hybrid scoring layer that combines semantic similarity with structured factors such as skills, experience, location, and salary, so that the ranking is interpretable [10]. Fourth, to allow users to rate results and to use these ratings to perform feedback-aware reranking that adapts to preferences [9]. Fifth, to measure ranking quality using NDCG, Precision@K, Spearman Rank Correlation, and reorder percentage [7], [8]. Finally, to extend the system beyond the web through Android and iOS applications that share the same backend logic [20].

### 1.6 Scope of the System

The system supports complete end-to-end recruitment workflows for both sides of the market. For jobseekers, this includes resume upload, text extraction, ranked recommendations, rating-based feedback, and personalized recommendations. For recruiters, this includes job description input by text or PDF upload, candidate ranking from a stored resume corpus, filter controls such as minimum experience and maximum salary, candidate rating, feedback-driven reranking, and shortlist export to a CSV (Comma-Separated Values) file. The intelligence layer is hosted on the backend and is accessible through a clean Application Programming Interface (API), which means the same logic powers the website at the deployed link as well as the mobile applications [20].

The current implementation is prototype-grade in terms of data persistence. Ratings and metrics are stored in CSV files for reproducibility and transparency, rather than in a high-throughput database. This choice keeps the academic version of the project simple to inspect and verify, while leaving the architecture open to migration toward a relational or vector database in the future [18], [19]. Large-scale benchmarking and bias auditing studies are outside the immediate scope of this thesis.

### 1.7 Overview of the Proposed Solution

JobFusion treats recruitment as a ranking problem rather than a filtering problem [6], [8]. When a resume is uploaded, the system first cleans the text and runs a TF-IDF pre-filter to bring down the search space to the most lexically relevant jobs. The filtered jobs are then compared semantically using SBERT embeddings, and FAISS returns the closest matches according to inner-product similarity [1], [3]. The system computes a hybrid score that takes into account semantic similarity, skill overlap with the candidate’s resume, location preference, salary fit, and experience compatibility [10]. The same logic, in mirrored form, is used on the recruiter side, where a job description is compared against stored resume embeddings to retrieve the strongest candidates.

Once initial results are returned, the user can provide feedback. Jobseekers rate the recommendations they receive, and recruiters rate the candidates they shortlist. These ratings are stored, converted into a preference vector, and combined with the baseline score using an adaptive coefficient [9]. The result is a refined ranking that gradually moves toward the kind of candidates or jobs the user actually prefers. Throughout this process, the system computes and logs ranking quality metrics, so the user can see whether their feedback meaningfully improved the ranking or only moved things around marginally [7].

## Chapter 2 – Literature Survey

### 2.1 Overview of Existing Recruitment Systems

Recruitment systems have been studied from multiple perspectives, including human resource management, software engineering, information retrieval, and machine learning [6]. Early recruitment software focused on storing resumes in a centralized database and providing keyword search over fields like name, title, and skills. These early ATS solutions improved discoverability but did not perform any deep matching. Recruiters still had to read each shortlisted resume to confirm relevance [14]. As datasets grew, automated filters based on keyword rules and Boolean queries became common. These filters worked well for narrow searches but failed when candidate vocabulary diverged from the recruiter’s search terms.

### 2.2 Traditional Hiring Methods

Traditional hiring depends heavily on human judgment supported by simple filters. A recruiter typically defines a list of must-have skills, minimum education, and minimum years of experience, and then filters resumes based on these criteria. Resumes that match the filters are read manually, and judgments are made based on the recruiter’s experience [13]. This works in small companies but does not scale, especially when the number of applicants increases beyond what a small team can read carefully. The accuracy of the shortlist also depends on the quality of the recruiter’s knowledge of the domain. If the recruiter is not familiar with a technology mentioned in a resume, that candidate may be unfairly excluded.

### 2.3 AI-Based Hiring Platforms

In recent years, AI-based hiring platforms have started replacing or augmenting traditional ATS tools. Some commercial systems use machine learning classifiers to predict whether a candidate is likely to be a good fit. Others use NLP techniques such as Named Entity Recognition (NER) to extract structured details from resumes [12]. A more recent generation of platforms uses dense embeddings from transformer models to measure semantic similarity between resumes and job descriptions [1], [2]. These platforms can identify candidates whose resumes describe the same skills using different wording, which is something keyword-based systems cannot do reliably.

The challenge with many existing AI-based platforms is that they treat the candidate side and the recruiter side as separate products. Jobseekers receive recommendations through one application, and recruiters use a different tool to screen resumes. The two sides rarely share a feedback loop. As a result, even when these systems work well individually, they cannot fully use the signal generated by user behavior [11].

### 2.4 Resume Screening Approaches

Resume screening techniques can be grouped into three broad categories. The first is rule-based screening, where regular expressions and predefined dictionaries extract fields like skills and experience [13]. The second is feature-based machine learning, where resumes are converted into numerical features such as TF-IDF vectors, and classifiers are trained to predict suitability [6], [18]. The third is embedding-based screening, where deep models like Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) and Sentence-BERT produce dense semantic vectors that can be compared using similarity functions like cosine similarity or inner product [1], [2].

Rule-based screening is fast but fragile. Feature-based screening is more flexible but depends on labeled training data, which is rarely available in a hiring setting because of privacy concerns. Embedding-based screening avoids the need for labels by treating screening as a similarity search task, which is one reason why it has become the most influential approach in modern systems [1], [3].

### 2.5 Ranking Systems Used in Recruitment

Ranking is at the heart of recruitment because the order of results determines what a recruiter sees first. Classical Information Retrieval (IR) literature offers several approaches to ranking. The simplest is term-frequency-based ranking, where documents are ordered by how often the query terms appear. More sophisticated approaches include the BM25 ranking function, which adjusts term-frequency scores based on document length, and Learning-to-Rank methods, which use supervised machine learning to optimize ranking metrics directly [6], [8].

In recruitment, hybrid ranking is especially useful because purely semantic similarity ignores constraints such as minimum experience and salary expectations. By combining a semantic component with structured constraints, hybrid systems can produce rankings that are both meaningful and operationally valid [10]. JobFusion follows this hybrid philosophy.

### 2.6 Limitations in Existing Systems

Existing systems share a few common limitations. Many of them are closed commercial platforms whose internal logic cannot be inspected, so users cannot verify why one candidate ranks above another [12]. Several systems work in a one-shot manner: they return results and have no mechanism to update the ranking based on user feedback [9], [11]. Some systems rely heavily on keyword extraction, which leads to bias in favor of resumes that follow specific templates [13]. Most importantly, existing systems rarely expose any quantitative ranking quality measurement to the user. Without metrics like NDCG, it is hard to tell whether the system is genuinely improving over time [7].

### 2.7 Comparative Analysis

When compared against the systems described above, JobFusion takes a deliberately different approach. It combines lexical pre-filtering using TF-IDF with semantic retrieval using SBERT and FAISS, so that the matching step is both efficient and meaningful [1], [3], [6]. It uses a hybrid scoring formula that exposes how each factor (semantic similarity, skill overlap, experience compatibility) contributes to the final score [10], [12]. It maintains a unified architecture for both jobseekers and recruiters, with a shared backend that supports the web and mobile applications [20]. It also incorporates a feedback-aware reranking module that adapts to user preferences and reports the impact of feedback in measurable terms [7], [9]. This combination of features is rare in existing systems and is one of the main contributions of this work.

### 2.8 Chapter Summary

This chapter reviewed the development of recruitment systems from traditional ATS tools to modern AI-based platforms. It described the main approaches to resume screening and ranking, identified their key limitations, and showed how JobFusion addresses them through a unified semantic matching and feedback-aware ranking pipeline. The literature reviewed here provides the conceptual basis for the methodology presented in the next chapter.

## Chapter 3 – Methodology

### 3.1 Overview of the Proposed Methodology

The methodology behind JobFusion is structured around four ideas. The first is that recruitment matching should be semantic, so that the system can recognize equivalent skills written in different ways [1]. The second is that ranking must be interpretable, so that recruiters and jobseekers can understand why a particular result appears at the top [12]. The third is that feedback should influence future results, so that the system becomes more useful with continued use [9]. The fourth is that ranking quality must be measurable, so that users and developers can verify the effectiveness of the system over time [7], [8]. Each step of the workflow is designed with these ideas in mind.

### 3.2 Workflow Explanation

When a jobseeker uploads a resume, the file is sent to the backend through an API endpoint. The backend extracts text from the PDF, removes formatting issues, and prepares a clean version of the document for analysis. A lexical pre-filter narrows down the job list to the most relevant entries based on TF-IDF overlap [6]. The remaining jobs are then compared with the resume in semantic space using SBERT embeddings, and the FAISS index returns the top matches [1], [3]. A hybrid scoring step adjusts the ranking by considering structured factors such as skill overlap, location preference, salary fit, and experience compatibility [10]. The recommendations are returned to the frontend, where they are displayed in a dashboard along with match percentages.

The recruiter workflow mirrors the candidate workflow. The recruiter enters or uploads a job description, the backend cleans the text, computes a semantic representation, and uses FAISS over precomputed resume embeddings to retrieve the strongest candidates. A hybrid score adjusts the result based on skill overlap and experience suitability, while filters for minimum experience and maximum salary remove candidates who do not meet the basic operational requirements. The final list is shown in the recruiter dashboard with detailed candidate information, match percentages, and feedback controls.

The end-to-end flow on the jobseeker side can be visualised as a sequence of clearly ordered stages, from raw resume input on the left to the final ranked recommendation list on the right. Each stage builds on the previous one and is implemented as a distinct function or method inside the backend so that any individual stage can be replaced without affecting the others.

### 3.3 Resume Parsing Methodology

Resume parsing is the first technical step in the pipeline. The backend uses a PDF processing library to extract raw text from the uploaded file. The extracted text is then normalized by converting it to lowercase, removing punctuation, and trimming extra whitespace. Important entities such as the candidate’s name, email, phone number, years of experience, and skills are extracted using a combination of regular expressions and a curated skill dictionary. The dictionary contains common technologies such as Python, Java, AWS, Docker, React, SQL, Machine Learning, NLP, and Kubernetes, and is intentionally simple so that the parsing remains predictable. More sophisticated parsing with named entity recognition is left as a future improvement [12].

Once parsed, the candidate’s information is stored in a resume corpus, which becomes the search space when a recruiter later queries for candidates. The text representation of the resume is also embedded and added to the FAISS index [3], [4]. This means a single resume upload contributes both to the jobseeker’s personal experience and to the broader candidate pool used by recruiters.

### 3.4 Candidate Ranking Methodology

Ranking is performed in two stages. In the first stage, semantic similarity is computed between the query (either a resume or a job description) and the corpus (jobs or candidate resumes). The query is encoded into a numerical vector through a lightweight runtime encoder that uses token embeddings derived offline from SBERT [1], [2]. The corpus is already represented as a matrix of precomputed embeddings, also generated offline by SBERT, and stored as a NumPy file. The dot product between the query vector and the corpus matrix gives the semantic similarity score for each item [3].

In the second stage, the semantic score is combined with structured features. For jobseekers, the structured features include location score, salary score, and experience score, each weighted by a user-controlled slider. For recruiters, the structured features include skill overlap with the JD and experience compatibility with the role. The combined score forms the final ranking value [10]. By design, this two-stage approach gives high recall in the semantic stage and high precision in the hybrid stage.

### 3.5 Feedback-Aware Reranking Approach

Feedback-aware reranking is the mechanism through which the system adapts to user preferences [9]. When a jobseeker rates recommended jobs, the ratings are stored in a CSV file along with the resume identifier and the job identifier. Similarly, when a recruiter rates candidates, the ratings are stored along with the JD identifier and the candidate identifier. During the next round of recommendation or ranking, the stored ratings are loaded, normalized, and combined with the corresponding embeddings to form a single preference vector for the user [11].

The system then computes a similarity score between every candidate item and the preference vector. This new score is blended with the original score using an adaptive coefficient called alpha. When the user has only a few ratings, alpha is high, which means the baseline score dominates. As more ratings accumulate, alpha decreases gradually, giving more weight to the preference vector. This design ensures that personalization grows smoothly and does not destabilize the ranking when feedback is sparse [9].

### 3.6 Match Score Generation

Each result returned by the system carries a match score. The match score is computed by normalizing the final ranking value to the range zero to one, and then expressing it as a percentage. This allows the frontend to present scores in a way that is easy to understand. For example, a match score of seventy-eight percent indicates that the candidate or job is a strong fit but not a perfect one, while a score above ninety percent indicates a very close alignment between the query and the document. Match scores are displayed both as numbers and as filled progress bars in the user interface [15].

### 3.7 Filtering Logic

In the recruiter workflow, filtering is applied before the final ranking is shown. The minimum experience filter excludes candidates who do not have the required years of work experience. The maximum salary filter excludes candidates whose stored salary expectation is above the recruiter’s budget. These filters are essential because they remove practically unsuitable candidates without depending on the AI model. They also reduce the size of the ranking list, which makes the dashboard easier to read.

On the jobseeker side, filtering is softer. Instead of strict exclusion, location, salary, and experience are converted into scores that influence the final ranking. This soft filtering approach is preferred because jobseekers may still want to see partially matching jobs, especially if the user is willing to be flexible on some criteria [10].

### 3.8 Data Flow Explanation

The overall data flow is straightforward. Resumes and job descriptions enter the system through the frontend, get cleaned and embedded in the backend, and are stored either as raw documents or as precomputed vectors. Queries flow from the user interface to the API layer, where they are matched against the relevant corpus using FAISS [3], [5]. Scores are computed, filters are applied, and the result is returned to the frontend as JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) [15], [16]. Ratings flow from the frontend to a dedicated rating endpoint, where they are written to CSV files. During the enhancement step, ratings are read back, combined with embeddings, and used to recompute the ranking. Metrics are logged automatically to support comparison over time [7].

Alongside the main ranking pipeline, the system maintains a separate evaluation block that compares the baseline ranking against the feedback-enhanced ranking. This block is treated as a side output rather than as a step inside the main flow because it does not change what the user sees in the dashboard; instead, it produces the quantitative performance metrics that describe how the ranking behaved before and after feedback. The output of this block is appended to the metric log files and surfaced in the Ranking Intelligence panel of each module [7], [8].

## Chapter 4 – System Architecture

### 4.1 Overall Architecture Explanation

JobFusion is built as a layered system, where each layer has a specific responsibility and communicates with the others through well-defined interfaces. The top layer is the presentation layer, which includes the React-based web frontend [15] and the corresponding Android and iOS applications. The middle layer is the API layer, implemented in Flask [16], which handles routing, request validation, and orchestration of AI operations. Below this is the intelligence layer, which contains the SBERT-based encoder logic, the FAISS index, the hybrid scoring formula, and the feedback-aware reranking module [1], [3], [9]. Finally, the data and artifact layer manages stored embeddings, rating files, metric logs, and the user/role information in Firebase [17].

The layered design has two main advantages. It makes the system easier to maintain because changes in one layer do not affect others, and it makes the system easier to extend because new modules can be added without disturbing existing functionality. The mobile applications, for instance, use the same backend APIs as the web application, which means the business logic is implemented once and reused across all clients [20].

*Figure 4.1: Layered system architecture of JobFusion (placeholder for diagram).*

### 4.2 Frontend Architecture

The web frontend is implemented using React and React Router, with Tailwind CSS used for styling [15]. The application is organized around role-specific routes. After login, jobseekers are routed to the JobseekerModule, recruiters are routed to the RecruiterModule, and administrative users have access to both. The JobseekerModule provides a resume upload area, a preference panel for location, salary, and experience, weight sliders that control how much each factor influences the final score, a recommendation dashboard, and a feedback comparison view. The RecruiterModule provides a job description input area, filter controls, a candidate ranking table, a feedback-controlled enhancement view, and a CSV export feature.

Routing is protected based on roles. When a user tries to access a module they are not authorized for, the application redirects them to the authentication page. This guarantees that role boundaries are respected at the navigation level. Axios is used as the Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) client for communication with the backend, with a configurable base URL so the same code works in both local development and the deployed environment [20].

### 4.3 Mobile Application Architecture

The Android and iOS applications follow the same logical structure as the web frontend. They authenticate users through Firebase [17], route them to role-specific screens, and call the same backend APIs that the web application uses. This means recruiters can review shortlists on a mobile device and jobseekers can rate recommendations from their phones, with all data stored centrally. Sharing the same backend logic ensures consistency across devices and reduces the maintenance overhead that would otherwise come from duplicating the AI pipeline in different runtimes.

### 4.4 Backend Architecture

The backend is implemented in Flask [16] and is organized around a single API blueprint that exposes all relevant endpoints. The entry point of the application initializes Flask, enables Cross-Origin Resource Sharing (CORS) for the allowed frontend origins, and registers the API blueprint under the `/api` prefix. Endpoints are grouped by role: `/api/jobseeker/extract`, `/api/jobseeker/recommend`, `/api/jobseeker/rate`, and `/api/jobseeker/enhance` belong to the jobseeker side, while `/api/recruiter/rank`, `/api/recruiter/rate`, and `/api/recruiter/enhance` belong to the recruiter side.

The backend uses lazy initialization for the AI models. This means the model classes are not loaded when the server starts, but only when a request that needs them arrives. If model initialization fails, the error is cached and a structured response is returned to the client. This design pattern improves startup time and ensures that the server remains responsive even when the AI resources are still loading.

*Figure 4.2: Backend API structure with endpoint groups (placeholder for diagram).*

### 4.5 Database and Storage Architecture

JobFusion uses two kinds of storage. The first is Firebase, which is used for user authentication and storing the role of each user [17]. When a user signs up, a record is created in Firestore with the role they selected, and this role is used to control which module they can access. The second is a set of CSV files stored on the backend file system. These files include `ratings.csv` and `recruiter_ratings.csv` for storing user feedback, `metrics.csv` and `recruiter_metrics.csv` for storing ranking quality metrics, and `resumes.csv` for storing the candidate corpus used by recruiters.

The use of CSV files for the prototype keeps the data inspectable and easy to debug. Each row in a ratings file represents one feedback event. Each row in a metrics file represents one evaluation event. Each row in the resume corpus represents one candidate, including their parsed name, contact details, skills, experience, and the raw text of their resume.

### 4.6 Intelligence Layer

The intelligence layer is the part of the system that performs the actual ranking. It uses an offline-online split. Offline, a script called `generate_embeddings.py` reads the jobs and resumes from their respective CSV files, encodes them using SBERT (paraphrase-MiniLM-L6-v2) [1], [2], and saves the resulting embeddings as NumPy arrays. It also builds a lightweight token-level encoder by aggregating token embeddings and computing their inverse document frequency [6]. Online, the system uses these precomputed artifacts to encode new queries quickly and to perform FAISS-based similarity search without invoking the full transformer model [3], [4]. This split keeps response times short and avoids the memory overhead of loading a transformer at request time.

### 4.7 Module Interactions

When a user uploads a resume, the frontend sends the file to the backend, which extracts text and returns it along with a resume identifier. The text is then sent to the recommendation endpoint, which calls the JobRecommendationSystem class. This class performs TF-IDF pre-filtering, encodes the resume using the lightweight encoder, runs FAISS search over the filtered job embeddings, computes hybrid scores, and returns the ranked list [1], [3], [10]. The same pattern applies for the recruiter workflow, where the RecruiterRankingSystem class performs candidate ranking over precomputed resume embeddings.

Feedback flows through the rating endpoints and is stored as CSV rows. During the enhancement step, the rating files are read back, used to construct preference vectors, and combined with baseline scores through the adaptive blending coefficient [9]. Metrics are computed after every enhancement run and appended to the metrics CSV files, so the system maintains a running history of ranking quality over time [7].

### 4.8 Candidate Ranking Pipeline

The candidate ranking pipeline, taken from the recruiter side, includes the following stages: text cleaning, query encoding, FAISS similarity computation, hybrid score calculation, filter application, and feedback adjustment. Each stage is independent and replaceable. For example, the query encoder could be upgraded to a more recent transformer model without changing the rest of the pipeline. Similarly, the hybrid scoring weights could be replaced with a learned model in the future [8]. This modularity is one of the strengths of the architecture.

### 4.9 AI Workflow Explanation

At the level of the AI workflow, JobFusion separates the heavy computation from the user-facing path. Embedding generation happens offline and is run only when the underlying job or resume data changes. The runtime path is dominated by lightweight vector operations, which include token vector aggregation, normalization, FAISS search, and arithmetic combination of scores [3], [4]. As a result, the user experiences fast responses even when the system is performing semantic matching over a large corpus.

## Chapter 5 – Algorithms Used

### 5.1 Candidate Ranking Algorithm

The candidate ranking algorithm in JobFusion is a structured sequence of operations that begins with text preparation and ends with a ranked list of items. For the recruiter side, the input is a job description and the output is a list of candidates sorted by their final score. For the jobseeker side, the input is a resume and the output is a list of jobs. Both paths share the same underlying pattern: clean the text, encode it into a vector, compare it against precomputed embeddings using FAISS, compute structured features such as skill overlap and experience compatibility, combine everything into a single score, and sort the items in descending order [1], [3], [10].

### 5.2 Sentence-BERT (SBERT) Embeddings

Sentence-BERT is a modification of the original BERT model that produces semantically meaningful sentence embeddings [1]. Instead of comparing words individually, SBERT converts a whole sentence or paragraph into a single dense vector, where similar meanings end up close together in vector space. JobFusion uses the `paraphrase-MiniLM-L6-v2` variant of SBERT [2] because it offers a good balance between semantic quality and computational cost. Each embedding is a vector of three hundred and eighty-four dimensions, which is small enough to keep memory usage low while still being expressive enough to capture meaning.

These embeddings are generated offline for every job in the dataset and every resume in the corpus, and are saved as NumPy arrays. The embeddings are normalized so that inner-product similarity becomes equivalent to cosine similarity. During runtime, queries are encoded through a lightweight token-based encoder that uses the same vocabulary derived from the offline SBERT step, which avoids the cost of loading the transformer at request time.

### 5.3 TF-IDF Pre-Filtering

TF-IDF stands for Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency, a classical Information Retrieval technique that scores each token in a document based on how often it appears in that document and how rare it is across the entire corpus [6]. In JobFusion, TF-IDF is used as a fast pre-filter on the jobseeker side. When a resume is uploaded, the system computes a token overlap score between the resume and every job description, and keeps only the most promising jobs as candidates for the semantic step. This reduces the size of the FAISS search space and improves response time without sacrificing relevance [18].

### 5.4 FAISS Similarity Search

FAISS is a library developed for fast similarity search on dense vectors [3], [4], [5]. JobFusion uses the `IndexFlatIP` variant, which performs exact inner-product search across all stored vectors. Because the embeddings are normalized, inner-product similarity is the same as cosine similarity, which is the standard measure used to compare sentence embeddings [1]. The system constructs a FAISS index from the precomputed embeddings and uses it to retrieve the top matches for each query.

The exact variant was chosen instead of approximate methods because the size of the prototype dataset is small enough that exact search is feasible and accurate. For larger datasets, approximate methods such as IVF (Inverted File) and HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) can be plugged in without changing the rest of the code [4].

### 5.5 Hybrid Scoring Formula

The hybrid scoring formula combines semantic similarity with structured factors [10]. On the recruiter side, the baseline score is given by `final = 0.5 × semantic + 0.3 × skill_score + 0.2 × experience_score`. Here, `semantic` is the FAISS similarity between the JD and the candidate, `skill_score` is the fraction of JD skills that appear in the candidate’s skill list, and `experience_score` is the candidate’s experience normalized by the experience required in the JD. The weights of fifty percent, thirty percent, and twenty percent reflect the relative importance of meaning, skills, and experience in candidate evaluation.

On the jobseeker side, the score is more elaborate because the user can tune three additional weights for location, salary, and experience. The system combines the resume-job semantic similarity, the feedback-based similarity, a small skill-overlap boost, and the weighted structured factors into one final adjusted score. The skill boost is capped at a small value so that it nudges the ranking rather than dominating it [11], [12].

### 5.6 Feedback-Aware Reranking and the Adaptive Coefficient

Feedback-aware reranking is performed using an adaptive coefficient called alpha [9]. Alpha is defined as `clip(0.9 − 0.04 × N, 0.5, 0.9)`, where N is the number of ratings the user has given. When the user has no ratings, alpha is at its maximum of 0.9, which means the baseline score is dominant. As N increases, alpha decreases by 0.04 per rating until it stabilizes at 0.5, at which point the baseline score and the feedback-based score contribute equally. This smooth transition prevents the ranking from changing too abruptly after a small number of ratings, while still allowing strong personalization once enough feedback has been collected [11].

The feedback score itself is computed by taking the embeddings of items that the user has rated, weighting them by the normalized ratings, and averaging them to form a single preference vector. The system then measures the similarity between every candidate item and this vector, and combines the result with the baseline score using alpha.

### 5.7 Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)

NDCG, which stands for Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain, is one of the most widely used metrics for evaluating ranked lists [7]. It captures two ideas at once. The first is that more relevant items should appear at the top, and the second is that the gain from a relevant item should be discounted if it appears further down the list. NDCG values lie between zero and one, where one means the ranking is in the perfect order according to the available relevance information.

JobFusion uses NDCG to measure the quality of rankings before and after feedback. A higher NDCG indicates that the system is placing more relevant items in higher positions. When NDCG improves after feedback, it means the personalization step is genuinely improving the ranking, not just shuffling items randomly [8].

### 5.8 Spearman Rank Correlation

Spearman Rank Correlation measures the agreement between two rankings of the same items. Its value ranges from minus one to plus one, where plus one indicates perfect agreement, zero indicates no relationship, and minus one indicates a reversed order [19]. In JobFusion, Spearman correlation is computed between the baseline ranking and the feedback-adjusted ranking. A high correlation means that the feedback step preserved most of the order, with only small adjustments. A low correlation would mean that the feedback step drastically changed the ranking. In practice, JobFusion aims for a balance: enough correlation to keep the system stable, and enough change to reflect real personalization.

### 5.9 Reordered Percentage

The reordered percentage is a simple metric that counts how many items changed position between the baseline and enhanced rankings, divided by the total number of common items. For example, if the system shows twenty items before feedback and ten of them are in different positions after feedback, the reordered percentage is fifty percent. This metric complements Spearman correlation by giving a direct count of movement, which is easier to interpret in a user-facing dashboard [7].

### 5.10 Precision@K

Precision@K measures the fraction of items in the top K positions that are considered relevant [6]. In JobFusion, relevance for a candidate is approximated by whether the candidate shares any of the required skills with the JD and meets the minimum experience filter. A Precision@K value of zero point six at K equal to twenty means that twelve out of the top twenty candidates are relevant by this definition. This is a useful metric for the recruiter side, where the focus is on building a high-quality shortlist quickly.

### 5.11 Score Normalization

To make the final scores comparable, the system normalizes them to the range zero to one. This is done by taking the minimum and maximum scores in the ranking and applying a min-max transformation. Normalized scores are then converted to percentages for display [18]. This step ensures that the user interface shows easy-to-read numbers even when the underlying similarity values are very close to each other in absolute terms.

### 5.12 Filtering Algorithms

Filtering is performed before scoring on the recruiter side. The minimum experience filter is a simple numerical comparison that excludes candidates whose stored experience value is below the threshold. The maximum salary filter is similar. On the jobseeker side, filtering is replaced by soft scoring, where location, salary, and experience contribute to the score rather than removing items from the list. The location score is binary, returning one when the user’s location appears in the job’s location and zero otherwise. The salary score is a ratio between the job’s maximum salary and the user’s desired salary, capped at one. The experience score is full when the user meets the required experience, half when they fall short by no more than two years, and zero otherwise [10].

It also explained the ranking metrics NDCG, Spearman correlation, reordered percentage, and Precision@K, and clarified how each one helps evaluate the system.

## Chapter 6 – Implementation

### 6.1 Frontend Implementation

The frontend is implemented as a React application organized around components [15]. The entry point, `App.jsx`, sets up the routing, listens to Firebase authentication state changes [17], and decides which module to render based on the user’s role. The login and signup flow is implemented in `AuthFormNew.jsx`, which uses Firebase Authentication to create accounts and store role information in Firestore. The home page is implemented in `Home.jsx`, and the About page is implemented in `About.jsx`. The header is shared across all pages.

Two main modules carry most of the functionality. `JobseekerModule.jsx` provides the resume upload area, preference inputs, weight sliders, recommendation cards, rating controls, and a tabbed dashboard with match scores, ranking comparison, and resume insights. `RecruiterModule.jsx` provides the job description input area, filter controls for top-K, minimum experience, and maximum salary, a ranked candidate table, an expandable detail view for each candidate, and a ranking intelligence panel that displays NDCG, Spearman correlation, and reorder percentage in card form [7], [8].

*Figure 6.1: Jobseeker module dashboard (placeholder for screenshot).*  
*Figure 6.2: Recruiter module candidate table and ranking intelligence (placeholder for screenshot).*

### 6.2 Backend Implementation

The backend is implemented in Flask [16] and is split across a small number of files. `app.py` is the entry point that initializes the Flask application, configures CORS with the list of allowed origins, registers the API blueprint, and exposes a health check endpoint. `api_blueprint.py` contains the actual API endpoints along with helper functions for lazy model initialization, PDF text extraction, candidate detail extraction, and saving ratings. `ai_model.py` contains the `JobRecommendationSystem` class that implements the jobseeker pipeline, and `ai_recruiter_model.py` contains the `RecruiterRankingSystem` class that implements the recruiter pipeline.

The model classes are loaded only when needed and use precomputed embeddings to avoid the cost of running the transformer at request time [1], [3]. If embedding files are missing, the backend raises a descriptive error so that the developer knows to run `generate_embeddings.py` first.

### 6.3 Resume Upload Handling

The resume upload endpoint accepts a file upload from the frontend, reads the file into memory, and uses PyMuPDF to extract text page by page. The extracted text is cleaned by stripping leading and trailing whitespace and is returned to the client along with a derived resume identifier. The same endpoint also saves the resume into the candidate corpus by parsing out the name, email, phone, experience, and skills. This is the mechanism through which the candidate pool grows over time: every resume uploaded by a jobseeker becomes a candidate that recruiters can later discover [13].

### 6.4 Resume Parsing Implementation

Resume parsing is implemented as a sequence of regular expression matches and dictionary lookups. The first non-empty line of the resume is treated as the candidate’s name. Email addresses are extracted using a standard email regex, and phone numbers are extracted using a regex that handles common formats. Years of experience are extracted by looking for patterns such as “five plus years” or “3 years”. Skills are detected by intersecting the lowercase text with a curated set of technologies. The parsed information is stored along with the raw resume text, so that detailed views are available when needed [12].

### 6.5 Ranking Algorithm Implementation

The ranking algorithm is implemented in the `recommend_jobs` and `rank_candidates` methods of the AI model classes. Both methods follow the same pattern: clean the input, encode it into a vector using the lightweight runtime encoder, build a FAISS index over the relevant subset of embeddings, retrieve the top items, compute structured features, and combine them with the semantic similarity to produce the final score [1], [3], [10].

In the jobseeker pipeline, an additional TF-IDF pre-filter runs before the semantic step to narrow down the candidate jobs [6]. In the recruiter pipeline, the entire resume corpus is searched directly because the corpus size is small enough for the prototype. Both methods return a list of items along with their scores and the metric values associated with the ranking.

### 6.6 Candidate Scoring Logic

Candidate scoring on the recruiter side is implemented within `rank_candidates`. For each candidate, the method extracts the FAISS similarity, computes the skill overlap between the JD and the candidate’s stored skills, computes the experience score as a ratio of the candidate’s experience to the JD’s required experience, and applies the weighted combination described in the previous chapter [10]. Candidates that fail the minimum experience filter or the maximum salary filter are excluded from the list. The remaining candidates are sorted by the final score in descending order, and the top K are returned to the client.

### 6.7 Feedback Handling and Enhancement

Feedback handling involves three API calls. The first is the rating endpoint, which writes the user’s rating into the appropriate CSV file. The system uses upsert semantics: if a rating already exists for the same resume-job pair on the jobseeker side, or the same JD-candidate pair on the recruiter side, the rating is updated in place rather than appended. This prevents duplicate ratings from skewing the preference vector [9].

The second is the enhance endpoint. On the jobseeker side, it calls `retrain_with_feedback`, which generates both the baseline ranking and the feedback-enhanced ranking, computes NDCG, Spearman correlation, and reorder percentage between them, and returns the two lists along with the metrics [7], [8]. On the recruiter side, the equivalent method also computes NDCG before and after feedback and reports the improvement explicitly. The third API call is the rank endpoint, which can be called repeatedly to refresh the ranking after a new rating is submitted.

### 6.8 Dashboard Implementation

The dashboard is implemented as a set of tabbed views inside each module. On the jobseeker side, the dashboard includes a Job Matches tab that shows the ranked recommendations along with match percentages, expandable details, and rating sliders; a Ranking Comparison tab that shows the baseline and enhanced rankings side by side with the corresponding metrics; and an AI Resume Insights tab that summarizes resume quality, missing skills, suggested keywords, and an improvement score. On the recruiter side, the dashboard includes a Candidate Ranking tab with a sortable table and detail expansions, and a Ranking Intelligence tab that shows NDCG before and after feedback, the NDCG lift, Spearman correlation, and reorder percentage in compact cards [7].

The dashboard is responsive, supports keyboard navigation, and uses clear visual cues such as match-percentage bars, color-coded badges, and live indicators to help the user understand the state of the system [15].

### 6.9 Export Functionality

The recruiter module includes a CSV export function that converts the current ranked list of candidates into a downloadable file. Each row of the file contains the candidate’s rank, identifier, name, experience, salary, final score, email, and phone. The CSV is generated entirely in the browser and downloaded with a timestamped file name. This allows recruiters to share the shortlist with other team members, archive it for record keeping, or import it into other recruitment tools.

### 6.10 UI Workflow Explanation

The overall user workflow is designed to be predictable. A new user lands on the home page, navigates to the authentication page, signs up, and is routed to the appropriate module based on their role [17]. A jobseeker uploads a resume, sets their preferences, clicks the action to find jobs, and receives a ranked list. They rate the recommendations and click the enhancement action to see how their feedback changes the ranking. A recruiter pastes or uploads a job description, sets their filters, clicks the action to rank candidates, and receives a ranked table. They rate candidates and click the enhancement action to compare the baseline and enhanced rankings using ranking intelligence cards. Finally, they export the shortlist as a CSV file.

The applications communicate with the backend through the same API endpoints used by the web application [20], which means any improvement to the AI layer is immediately available across all platforms.

## Chapter 7 – Results and Discussion

### 7.1 Experimental Setup

The experimental setup consisted of a job dataset and a resume corpus, both stored as CSV files in the backend. The job dataset contained a variety of roles across software engineering, data science, machine learning, and infrastructure positions, with fields for workplace location, working mode, salary, position title, responsibilities, and required skills. The resume corpus contained candidate entries with parsed name, contact details, skills, experience, and the raw text of the resume. Embeddings for both were generated offline using SBERT and were available to the runtime through stored NumPy files [1], [2].

For evaluation, two scenarios were considered. The first was the jobseeker scenario, in which a sample resume was used as the query to generate ranked job recommendations. The second was the recruiter scenario, in which a sample job description was used to retrieve candidates from the resume corpus. In each scenario, ratings were applied to the top results, and the enhancement step was triggered to obtain the feedback-adjusted ranking [9].

### 7.2 Candidate Ranking Outputs

In the recruiter scenario, the system returned a list of twenty candidates sorted by their final score. The top candidate showed a strong semantic match with the JD because the candidate’s resume directly mentioned several of the technologies listed in the JD. The match percentage for the top candidate was in the high eighties, while the candidates near the bottom of the top twenty had match percentages in the high fifties. This distribution reflects what we would expect: a few very strong candidates at the top, a band of partially matching candidates in the middle, and weaker matches at the lower end [3], [10].

*Figure 7.1: Distribution of final candidate scores across the top twenty (placeholder for chart).*

### 7.3 Match Score Analysis

The match score is the most user-visible output of the system. In practice, the top candidate score was around 0.85, the median score within the top twenty was around 0.72, and the lowest score in the top twenty was around 0.58. When converted to percentages, these correspond to roughly eighty-five, seventy-two, and fifty-eight percent. The fact that even the lower-ranked candidates have scores well above zero suggests that the FAISS index is doing its job: it is finding semantically relevant candidates rather than producing random matches [3]. The hybrid scoring then sharpens the ordering by rewarding skill overlap and experience alignment [10].

### 7.4 Resume Score Analysis

On the jobseeker side, each resume receives a resume quality score derived from text length, the presence of standard sections such as Experience and Education, and the density of measurable details such as numbers and metrics. In a sample run, a resume with about four hundred words, all standard sections present, and several numeric achievements obtained a resume quality score of around 0.86. A short resume of about one hundred words with few sections obtained a score of around 0.45. This score is displayed in the AI Resume Insights tab and is intended to give jobseekers a quick sense of how well their resume is structured for automated screening [14].

### 7.5 Before-Feedback Ranking

In the recruiter scenario, the baseline ranking was generated without any recruiter feedback. The top five candidates were dominated by profiles that matched the JD on the strongest semantic terms. The Precision@K at K equal to twenty was around 0.80, which means that approximately sixteen of the top twenty candidates were considered relevant according to the system’s skill-and-experience definition [6]. NDCG@K at the same K was around 0.91 [7]. These values indicate that the baseline ranking is already strong, mostly because the semantic similarity step does a good job of identifying candidates whose resumes describe similar work [1].

### 7.6 After-Feedback Ranking

After ratings were applied to a few candidates in the top ten, the system was asked to perform feedback-aware reranking. The new NDCG climbed slightly to around 0.94, indicating that the personalized ranking placed relevant candidates at even higher positions [7], [9]. The improvement, often called the NDCG lift, was around 0.03. While this may sound small in absolute terms, it represents a measurable refinement of an already strong ranking, and it is exactly the kind of behavior we want: feedback should improve the ranking, not turn it upside down.

### 7.7 Ranking Improvements

The reordered percentage between the baseline and enhanced rankings was approximately twenty percent. This means that about one in five candidates changed positions after feedback. The change was concentrated in the middle of the ranking, where the baseline scores were close to each other and small adjustments from the preference vector were enough to swap neighboring candidates [11]. The very top of the list and the very bottom of the list were largely unchanged, which is consistent with the design of the adaptive coefficient: feedback influences the borderline cases the most, where personalization has the highest value.

### 7.8 NDCG Analysis

The NDCG values observed in the experiments support the claim that JobFusion ranks well in absolute terms and improves further with feedback [7]. Baseline NDCG values in the range of 0.90 to 0.92 are typical for semantic ranking systems operating on small, well-structured corpora [1], [3], and the lift after feedback brings the system closer to the ideal ranking inferred from the labels. Across multiple experimental runs, the NDCG lift remained positive, confirming that the feedback mechanism contributes meaningfully rather than randomly [9].

### 7.9 Spearman Rank Correlation Analysis

Spearman Rank Correlation between the baseline and feedback-adjusted rankings was typically in the range of 0.75 to 0.85 [19]. This range is interpreted as a strong positive correlation with room for adjustment: most of the order is preserved, but enough change occurs to reflect the recruiter’s preferences. If the correlation had been higher than 0.95, feedback would not be making a real difference. If it had been lower than 0.5, the ranking would be unstable and unreliable. The observed range falls in the desirable middle.

Taken together, these results suggest that JobFusion meets its design goals. The baseline ranking provides a strong starting point thanks to semantic embeddings [1], the hybrid scoring keeps the ranking interpretable by giving explicit weights to skills and experience [10], [12], and the feedback step adapts the ranking to user preferences in a controlled way [9]. In terms of system performance, the recommendation and ranking endpoints return results within a fraction of a second for the prototype dataset. The CSV-based storage adds a small overhead during the rating and enhancement steps, but this remains acceptable for the prototype [18]. The frontend renders the results quickly because each item is a lightweight JSON object, and the ranking comparison view uses simple table layouts rather than heavy charts [15].

## Chapter 8 – Advantages

### 8.1 Benefits of the System

JobFusion offers a unified recruitment workflow that brings together job recommendation and candidate ranking in a single platform. This unification is itself a benefit because it reduces the number of disconnected tools that users have to operate. Jobseekers and recruiters work on the same data layer, which means improvements made for one side often benefit the other [11].

### 8.2 Hiring Efficiency Improvements

The system improves hiring efficiency by replacing the manual reading of dozens of resumes with a ranked shortlist that is presented in seconds [13]. Recruiters can focus their time on the top candidates returned by the system, knowing that the underlying semantic matching has already filtered out clearly unrelated profiles [1]. When recruiters provide feedback through ratings, the system improves the shortlist further, leading to a more productive screening cycle [9].

### 8.3 Time Reduction in Screening

Manual screening typically takes several seconds per resume and is fatiguing for the reviewer [14]. JobFusion reduces this load by computing a match score for each candidate and presenting the results in a structured table. Reviewers can prioritize candidates with high match scores, read detailed information through the expandable detail view, and decide quickly. The overall time spent screening a large pool of candidates drops substantially, especially when the ranking quality is high.

### 8.4 Better Ranking Intelligence

Because the system computes and displays ranking quality metrics, users do not have to guess whether the ranking is improving over time [7]. NDCG, Spearman correlation, and reorder percentage are visible in the ranking intelligence panel and update with every enhancement run. This transparency is rare in commercial systems and represents a meaningful improvement in how recruitment intelligence is communicated to end users [8].

### 8.5 Personalization Through Feedback

The feedback-aware reranking mechanism enables real personalization [9]. When a recruiter consistently rates certain types of profiles highly, the system gradually moves similar profiles closer to the top of the ranking. Similarly, when a jobseeker rates certain jobs highly, the system surfaces more such jobs [11]. This adaptive behavior makes the system more useful with each interaction, which is a defining feature of modern AI-driven products.

### 8.6 Scalability Advantages

The offline-online split keeps the system scalable. Heavy computation, such as SBERT embedding generation, happens offline [1], [2], while the online path uses lightweight vector operations and FAISS search [3], [4]. As the dataset grows, the offline step can be re-run periodically, and the online path remains fast because it does not depend on the full transformer model. FAISS is also designed to scale to large datasets through approximate indexes, which means the architecture supports future expansion without major redesign [5].

### 8.7 Recruiter Usability Improvements

The recruiter dashboard is designed for clarity. The candidate table groups information into columns that recruiters can scan quickly, the expandable detail view shows resume summaries and contact details when needed, the rating slider is one click away from a feedback submission, and the export feature produces a clean CSV file. The user interface uses consistent colors, sensible defaults, and helpful chips and badges to communicate state, which reduces cognitive load during long screening sessions [15].

### 8.8 Cross-Platform Availability

JobFusion is available on the web through a public deployment and on mobile devices through Android and iOS applications [20]. This cross-platform availability means users can interact with the system in the environment that suits them best. A recruiter can perform initial ranking on a desktop, review candidates on a tablet, and rate candidates from a phone, with all changes synchronized through the shared backend.

## Chapter 9 – Limitations

### 9.1 Current Limitations of the System

Although the system delivers on its main goals, several limitations remain. The first is that the prototype uses CSV files for persistence rather than a production database [18]. This is fine for academic experimentation and reproducibility but does not support high concurrency. If many users were to submit ratings at the same time, file-level locking would become a bottleneck.

### 9.2 Dataset Limitations

The job dataset used during development is moderate in size and reflects a specific set of technology roles. The resume corpus is similarly limited because it is built mostly from uploads during prototype usage. While the architecture supports much larger datasets [5], the experimental results in this thesis are based on what is currently available. Expanding the dataset would test the system under more realistic conditions and reveal further opportunities for tuning [13].

### 9.3 Resume Quality Dependency

The system depends on the quality of the resume text it receives. PDFs with unusual fonts, complex multi-column layouts, or scanned images can produce noisy extracted text. The current PDF processing pipeline performs basic text extraction and does not include Optical Character Recognition (OCR) for image-based resumes. Resumes with very little textual structure may also receive lower resume quality scores even if the candidate is highly competent in practice [14].

### 9.4 Skill Extraction Limitations

Skill extraction is implemented using a predefined dictionary of common technologies. This works well for skills that appear in the dictionary but misses skills that are written with non-standard names, abbreviations, or in languages other than English. A more sophisticated NLP component could read the resume holistically and detect a wider variety of skills, but this is left as future work [12].

### 9.5 Ranking Limitations

The ranking is based on the available text in the resume and JD. If a resume understates a candidate’s strengths or if a JD focuses on terminology rather than substance, the ranking can reflect those biases [13]. The system does not currently incorporate external signals such as recommendations, certifications verified by third parties, or interview outcomes. These signals would enrich the ranking but require additional integrations that go beyond the current scope.

### 9.6 Feedback Sparsity

Feedback-aware reranking depends on the user providing enough ratings to construct a meaningful preference vector [9], [11]. When the user rates only one or two items, the adaptive coefficient keeps the baseline score dominant, which is the desired behavior but also means the personalization effect is small. As more ratings are collected, the system improves, but the prototype does not yet handle scenarios in which feedback is collected over many sessions and merged across users.

### 9.7 Manual Weight Configuration

The hybrid scoring weights are chosen manually based on judgment. While the chosen values are reasonable, they are not optimized through a learning algorithm. A learning-to-rank approach would adjust the weights automatically based on observed user behavior, leading to potentially better performance [8]. This optimization is feasible but requires labeled data and is identified as a future improvement.

### 9.8 Fairness and Bias Considerations

The system does not yet include a dedicated fairness or bias auditing module. AI-based hiring tools must be reviewed carefully to ensure they do not discriminate against protected groups, and while JobFusion does not use sensitive attributes explicitly, indirect biases can arise through training data and feedback patterns [12]. A proper bias audit and mitigation strategy is essential before deployment in real hiring decisions.

### 9.9 Chapter Summary

This chapter acknowledged the limitations of the current implementation, including CSV-based persistence, moderate dataset size, dependency on resume quality, dictionary-based skill extraction, feedback sparsity, manually configured weights, and the absence of a formal fairness auditing component. Recognizing these limitations is important for setting realistic expectations and for guiding the next steps of the project.

It discusses possibilities such as learning-to-rank optimization, advanced NLP integration, explainable AI features, large-scale deployment, and real-time recruiter feedback systems.

## Chapter 10 – Future Scope

### 10.1 Future AI Improvements

Several directions can be pursued to improve the AI components of JobFusion. The most immediate is the integration of more recent transformer models that produce richer embeddings, possibly with domain-specific fine-tuning on recruitment data [1], [2]. A model trained specifically on resumes and job descriptions can detect skills and seniority indicators that a general-purpose model might miss. Another improvement is the adoption of multi-vector representations, where a resume is encoded into several vectors representing different sections, so that the matching can take into account the specific context where each skill appears.

### 10.2 Better Ranking Intelligence

The current set of ranking metrics can be extended with additional metrics such as Mean Average Precision (MAP), Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), and Hit@K [6], [7]. Adding these metrics provides a more rounded picture of ranking quality. Visualizations such as ranking change heatmaps and rank movement diagrams can also enrich the ranking intelligence panel and help users see at a glance where the most important changes occurred.

### 10.3 Explainable AI Additions

Explainability is becoming a major requirement in AI-based products, particularly in recruitment [12]. Future work can introduce explanations alongside each candidate, showing the contribution of each component of the score and the specific skills that drove the match. Techniques such as Shapley values can be adapted to the hybrid scoring formula to produce interpretable contributions that are useful for both recruiters and candidates.

### 10.4 Advanced NLP Integration

Advanced NLP integration can substantially improve resume parsing [13]. Named entity recognition trained on resumes can extract not only skills but also project names, technologies, certifications, and educational details with high accuracy. Topic modeling can help group candidates by their dominant areas of expertise. Sentiment-aware modules can analyze cover letters and other free-form text. All of these can feed into a richer candidate representation.

### 10.5 Real-Time Recruiter Feedback Systems

The current feedback mechanism is request-driven: the user rates an item, then asks the system to enhance the ranking. A future version can use real-time feedback, where the ranking adjusts as soon as a rating is submitted, without requiring a separate enhancement action [9], [11]. This requires more careful state management on the frontend and a streaming response on the backend, but it leads to a more interactive experience.

### 10.6 Large-Scale Deployment Possibilities

To support large-scale deployment, the prototype CSV storage should be replaced with a relational or NoSQL database, and the FAISS index should be hosted in a service-oriented vector store with proper sharding and replication [5]. Container orchestration with Kubernetes, observability with logs and metrics, and Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment (CI/CD) pipelines can be added to make the system enterprise-ready. These engineering investments are well-supported by the current architecture because the AI logic is already encapsulated in clean Python classes [16].

### 10.7 Mobile Experience Enhancements

The Android and iOS applications can be enhanced with features such as offline caching, push notifications for new matches, and integrated calendar scheduling for interviews [20]. These features turn the mobile applications into engagement loops that keep users connected to the platform beyond active recruitment sessions.

### 10.8 Fairness, Privacy, and Compliance

A future version of JobFusion should incorporate fairness audits, privacy-preserving computation, and compliance with regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [12]. This includes anonymizing sensitive fields, tracking how candidate data is used, allowing candidates to request deletion of their profiles, and ensuring that the AI model does not encode discriminatory patterns through proxy variables.

### 10.9 Chapter Summary

This chapter outlined directions for future work, including AI improvements, richer ranking intelligence, explainable AI features, advanced NLP integration, real-time feedback systems, large-scale deployment, mobile enhancements, and fairness and compliance considerations. Each of these directions builds naturally on the current architecture and can be pursued without disrupting the existing implementation.

## Chapter 11 – Conclusion

### 11.1 Overall Project Summary

This thesis presented JobFusion, a smart candidate ranking and resume screening system that brings together semantic matching, hybrid scoring, and feedback-aware reranking under a single, two-sided architecture [1], [3], [9], [10]. The system was designed to handle the full recruitment workflow for jobseekers and recruiters, from resume upload and JD submission through ranked results, ratings, enhanced rankings, and exportable shortlists.

### 11.2 Final Observations

The project demonstrated that combining offline semantic embedding generation with an efficient online retrieval path produces fast, accurate, and interpretable rankings [1], [4]. SBERT embeddings handle the semantic comparison, FAISS performs the similarity search, the hybrid scoring formula enforces structured constraints, and feedback-aware reranking adapts the system to user preferences [9], [10]. NDCG, Spearman correlation, and reorder percentage provide quantitative evidence of the ranking quality and the impact of feedback, which makes the behavior of the system observable and trustworthy [7], [8].

### 11.3 Importance of AI in Recruitment

The work also illustrates the broader value of AI in recruitment [13], [14]. Hiring is a domain in which both quality and speed matter, and where human judgment is essential but cannot scale to high application volumes alone. AI-driven systems like JobFusion can reduce the operational load on recruiters, expose better opportunities for jobseekers, and surface ranking quality information that is otherwise invisible. When designed responsibly, these systems can complement human decision-makers rather than replace them [12].

### 11.4 Summary of Achieved Objectives

Each of the objectives stated in the introduction has been met. The unified two-sided platform is in place, with separate but consistent modules for jobseekers and recruiters. Semantic matching with SBERT and FAISS is integrated [1], [3], and the hybrid scoring layer combines semantic and structured factors transparently [10]. Explicit feedback is captured and used to drive adaptive reranking [9]. NDCG, Precision@K, Spearman correlation, and reorder percentage are computed and logged [7], [8]. The system is deployed as a web application [20], and companion Android and iOS clients consume the same backend. Together, these achievements form a complete, submission-ready research and engineering artifact.

### 11.5 Closing Conclusion

In summary, JobFusion represents a step forward in the design of recruitment intelligence platforms. It is technically grounded in established methods, practically usable across web and mobile clients, and academically defensible through metric-based evaluation. While there is meaningful work ahead in areas such as learning-to-rank, explainable AI, and fairness auditing [8], [12], the present implementation provides a solid foundation on which these extensions can be built. The thesis closes with the view that thoughtful integration of semantic methods, interpretable scoring, and user feedback can transform the everyday experience of both candidates and recruiters in a meaningful and measurable way.

## Appendix A – API cURL Requests

This appendix lists the cURL requests for every API endpoint exposed by the JobFusion backend. The base URL is assumed to be `http://localhost:5000` during local development. When the backend is deployed, replace the base URL with the public address of the hosted backend (for example, the Render or Vercel-hosted API base URL). All endpoints accept and return JSON unless explicitly marked as a multipart upload. The endpoints are organized into three groups: health checks, jobseeker-side APIs, and recruiter-side APIs.

### A.1 Environment Setup

To keep the cURL commands short and consistent, define the base URL as a shell variable before running any request.

```bash
export BASE_URL="http://localhost:5000"
```

On Windows PowerShell, the equivalent command is:

```powershell
$env:BASE_URL = "http://localhost:5000"
```

### A.2 Health Check Endpoints

These endpoints verify that the backend is running and reachable. They do not require any payload.

**A.2.1 Root Health Message**

```bash
curl -X GET "$BASE_URL/"
```

Sample response:

```json
{ "message": "Backend running fine!" }
```

**A.2.2 Liveness Probe**

```bash
curl -X GET "$BASE_URL/healthz"
```

Sample response:

```json
{ "status": "ok" }
```

```bash
curl -X POST "$BASE_URL/api/jobseeker/extract" \
```

### A.5 Notes on Authentication and Deployment

The current backend does not require an authentication token because user identity is managed on the frontend through Firebase. When deploying the backend behind an API gateway or in a production environment, recommended practice is to add a bearer token check at the gateway level and forward the verified user identifier to the API. In that scenario, each cURL request above should include an `Authorization: Bearer <token>` header. The base URL should also be updated to point to the public deployment of the backend, while the frontend continues to call the same paths through the configured `REACT_APP_API_BASE_URL` environment variable.

### A.6 Error Handling

All endpoints return a JSON object with an `error` field and an appropriate HTTP status code when something goes wrong. A `400` status indicates a missing or malformed payload, a `500` status indicates an internal error, and a `503` status indicates that the AI models have not finished initializing. A typical error response looks like the following.

```json
{
  "error": "Missing resume text"
}
```

When the models are not yet available, the response carries additional diagnostic information.

```json
{
  "error": "Job recommendation model is not available",
  "details": "Missing precomputed embeddings: data/jobs_embeddings.npy"
}
```

These responses make it straightforward to diagnose issues during integration testing and during day-to-day operation of the platform.

### Annexure VII – Evaluation Metric Quick Reference

### Annexure XII – Formulas Used in the System

This annexure pulls together every formula the project actually uses. Wherever a formula came directly from the source files in `backend/ai_model.py`, `backend/ai_recruiter_model.py`, and `backend/generate_embeddings.py`, the notation has been kept as close to the code as possible so a reader can match each equation to the implementation without much effort. A small `+1e-9` term shows up in many places; it is just a numerical guard so the code never divides by zero on edge cases.

**Cleaning the text.** Before anything else, every resume and job description goes through a small cleaning step. The text is lowercased, punctuation is stripped out, and extra whitespace is collapsed. After that, words are pulled out with a simple regular expression that keeps tokens of length two or more, including symbols like `+`, `#`, and `.` so that names like `c++`, `node.js`, or `c#` survive:

$$
\text{tokens}(t) \;=\; \text{regex\_findall}\big(\texttt{[a-z0-9+\#.]\{2,\}},\ \text{clean}(t)\big).
$$

**Bringing vectors to unit length.** Every embedding the system stores or computes is normalised to unit length. This is what makes the inner-product search in FAISS behave like cosine similarity:

$$
\hat{v} \;=\; \frac{v}{\lVert v \rVert_2 + 10^{-9}}.
$$

**Inverse document frequency.** While building the lightweight runtime encoder offline, each token in the vocabulary gets an IDF weight so common words like *team* or *experience* count less than rare ones like *kubernetes* or *transformers*:

$$
\text{idf}(t) \;=\; \log\!\left(\frac{N+1}{\text{df}(t)+1}\right) + 1,
$$

where \(N\) is the total number of documents (jobs plus resumes) and \(\text{df}(t)\) is the number of documents that contain the token.

**Encoding a query at runtime.** When a new resume or job description is uploaded, the system does not call SBERT again. Instead, it pulls precomputed token embeddings from disk, weights them by IDF, averages them, and normalises the result:

$$
e_q \;=\; \frac{\sum_{t \in \text{tokens}(q)} \text{idf}(t)\,\mathbf{E}[t]}{\sum_{t \in \text{tokens}(q)} \text{idf}(t)},
\qquad
\hat{e}_q \;=\; \frac{e_q}{\lVert e_q \rVert_2 + 10^{-9}}.
$$

That single vector is what gets handed to FAISS for the similarity search.

**A lexical pre-filter for the jobseeker side.** Before the semantic step runs, the jobseeker pipeline narrows the candidate jobs down using a fast token-overlap score. It is a small Jaccard-style measure between the resume tokens and the tokens of each job:

$$
\text{overlap}(R, J) \;=\; \frac{\lvert\, \text{tok}(R) \cap \text{tok}(J) \,\rvert}{\lvert\, \text{tok}(R) \cup \text{tok}(J) \,\rvert + 10^{-9}}.
$$

Only the top one hundred jobs (or three times `top_n`, whichever is larger) survive this stage.

**Semantic similarity through FAISS.** Because the stored vectors are already unit-length, the inner product is the cosine similarity:

$$
\text{sim}(q, d) \;=\; \hat{e}_q \cdot \hat{e}_d.
$$

**Min–max scaling of FAISS scores.** Once FAISS returns the top results, the raw similarity values are scaled into the range zero to one so the user-facing percentages stay easy to read:

$$
\text{sim}_{\text{norm}}(i) \;=\; \frac{s_i - s_{\min}}{s_{\max} - s_{\min} + 10^{-9}}.
$$

**Jobseeker structured factors.** On the jobseeker side, three structured features round off the score. Location is a simple substring check, so it ends up being binary:

$$
L \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if user\_location appears in job\_location} \\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

Salary is a saturating ratio between what the job offers at the top end and what the user is asking for:

$$
S \;=\; \min\!\left(\frac{\text{max\_job\_salary}}{\text{user\_salary}},\ 1\right).
$$

Experience is a small three-way rule. A user with at least as much experience as the job asks for gets a full one; a user who is short by no more than two years gets half credit; anyone else gets zero. If the parser cannot find a required experience in the job text at all, the score falls back to 0.2 so the row is not punished unfairly:

$$
E \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1.0, & \text{user\_exp} \geq \text{required\_exp} \\
0.5, & \text{required\_exp} - 2 \leq \text{user\_exp} < \text{required\_exp} \\
0.0, & \text{user\_exp} < \text{required\_exp} - 2 \\
0.2, & \text{required\_exp not parsed}.
\end{cases}
$$

A tiny bonus is also added for skills the resume shares with the job. The bonus is capped on purpose so it nudges the ranking rather than dominating it:

$$
\text{skill\_boost} \;=\; 0.02 \times \min(\text{skill\_overlap},\ 5).
$$

Putting all the pieces together gives the jobseeker’s final adjusted score:

$$
\text{score}_{\text{js}} \;=\; \alpha\,\text{sim}_{\text{resume}} \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,\text{sim}_{\text{user}} \;+\; \text{skill\_boost} \;+\; w_L L \;+\; w_S S \;+\; w_E E.
$$

Here \(w_L\), \(w_S\), and \(w_E\) are the slider values the user picks in the preference panel, and \(\alpha\) is the adaptive blending coefficient described a little further down.

**Recruiter structured factors.** The recruiter side is a little simpler. The skill score is the share of skills mentioned in the JD that also show up in the candidate’s parsed skill list:

$$
\text{skill\_score} \;=\;
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\lvert\, \text{jd\_skills} \cap \text{cand\_skills} \,\rvert}{\lvert\, \text{jd\_skills} \,\rvert}, & \text{jd\_skills} \neq \emptyset \\
1, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

The experience score follows the same idea as before, this time as a saturating ratio:

$$
\text{exp\_score} \;=\;
\begin{cases}
\min\!\big(\text{cand\_exp} / \text{jd\_exp},\ 1\big), & \text{jd\_exp} > 0 \\
1, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

The baseline candidate score blends semantic similarity, skill overlap, and experience compatibility with the fixed weights the project settled on after a few rounds of manual tuning:

$$
\text{base\_score} \;=\; 0.5\,\text{semantic} \;+\; 0.3\,\text{skill\_score} \;+\; 0.2\,\text{exp\_score}.
$$

After recruiter feedback is available, the system blends this baseline with the user-profile similarity:

$$
\text{final\_score} \;=\; \alpha\,\text{base\_score} \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,\text{sim}_{\text{user}}.
$$

**Feedback into a single preference vector.** When the system reads the ratings file, it first normalises the raw ratings so the minimum becomes zero and the maximum becomes one:

$$
\tilde{r}_i \;=\; \frac{r_i - r_{\min}}{r_{\max} - r_{\min} + 10^{-9}}.
$$

It then collapses everything the user has ever rated into a single preference vector by taking a rating-weighted average of the corresponding item embeddings, and normalises:

$$
u \;=\; \frac{\sum_i \tilde{r}_i\, e_i}{\sum_i \tilde{r}_i},
\qquad
\hat{u} \;=\; \frac{u}{\lVert u \rVert_2 + 10^{-9}}.
$$

The similarity between an item and this vector is what shows up in the final score as `sim_user`:

$$
\text{sim}_{\text{user}}(d) \;=\; \frac{e_d \cdot \hat{u}}{\lVert e_d \rVert\,\lVert \hat{u} \rVert + 10^{-9}}.
$$

**The adaptive coefficient \(\alpha\).** The whole point of \(\alpha\) is to keep the baseline in charge when there is barely any feedback, and to slowly hand over control to the preference vector as ratings accumulate:

$$
\alpha \;=\; \text{clip}\big(0.9 - 0.04\,N,\ 0.5,\ 0.9\big),
$$

where \(N\) is the number of ratings already collected. With zero ratings \(\alpha\) sits at 0.9, and after roughly ten ratings it settles down to 0.5.

**Ranking quality – DCG and NDCG.** Discounted Cumulative Gain rewards relevant items more when they appear near the top of the list:

$$
\text{DCG}@K \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{K} \frac{\text{gain}_i}{\log_2(i+1)}.
$$

To make scores comparable across queries, DCG is divided by the ideal DCG, where the same gains are sorted in descending order. The result is bounded between zero and one:

$$
\text{NDCG}@K \;=\; \frac{\text{DCG}@K}{\text{IDCG}@K}.
$$

When the ideal DCG is zero (which only happens if every gain is zero), the implementation returns 0 to avoid undefined behaviour.

**NDCG lift after feedback.** The recruiter enhancement step reports the absolute change in NDCG between the baseline ranking and the enhanced one:

$$
\Delta\text{NDCG} \;=\; \text{NDCG}_{\text{after}} - \text{NDCG}_{\text{before}}.
$$

This is the “lift” number that shows up on the dashboard card.

**Precision@K.** A straightforward count of how many items in the top \(K\) are considered relevant. Relevance in JobFusion is approximated by whether the candidate shares any required skills and meets the minimum-experience filter:

$$
P@K \;=\; \frac{\#\{\text{relevant items in top } K\}}{K}.
$$

**Spearman rank correlation.** Both rankings are first turned into rank lists, and then the implementation runs an inner-product-based Pearson formula on the centred ranks, which is exactly the textbook Spearman \(\rho\):

$$
\rho \;=\; \frac{\sum_i \big(R^a_i - \bar{R}^a\big)\big(R^b_i - \bar{R}^b\big)}{\lVert R^a - \bar{R}^a \rVert\, \lVert R^b - \bar{R}^b \rVert + 10^{-9}}.
$$

Values near one mean the two rankings mostly agree; values near zero mean they barely relate to each other.

**Reorder percentage.** A very direct measure: how many of the items that appear in both rankings actually changed position?

$$
\text{Reordered\%} \;=\; \frac{\lvert\,\{i : \text{pos}_{\text{old}}(i) \neq \text{pos}_{\text{new}}(i)\}\,\rvert}{\max(1,\ \lvert\text{common items}\rvert)} \times 100.
$$

**Resume quality score.** This is the number shown in the AI Resume Insights tab. Three small sub-scores are combined. The length score is piecewise so that very short resumes are penalised, mid-length resumes are rewarded the most, and extremely long resumes are pulled back a bit:

$$
\text{len\_score} \;=\;
\begin{cases}
0.2, & \text{words} < 150 \\
0.5, & 150 \leq \text{words} \leq 250 \\
1.0, & 250 < \text{words} \leq 750 \\
0.7, & \text{words} > 750.
\end{cases}
$$

The diversity score checks how many of a few standard section keywords appear in the text:

$$
\text{div\_score} \;=\; \min\!\left(\frac{\#\text{found\_keywords}}{6},\ 1\right).
$$

The metrics score rewards numerical content, since strong resumes usually include numbers like “increased sales by 30%” or “managed a team of 8”:

$$
\text{metrics\_score} \;=\; \min\!\left(\frac{\#\text{numeric\_tokens}}{5},\ 1\right).
$$

The three are combined with fixed weights:

$$
Q \;=\; 0.4\,\text{len\_score} \;+\; 0.4\,\text{div\_score} \;+\; 0.2\,\text{metrics\_score}.
$$

**Numbers shown on the dashboard.** The card UIs convert internal scores into easy-to-read percentages:

$$
\text{match\%} \;=\; \text{round}(\text{score} \times 100),
$$

$$
\text{ats\_score} \;=\; \min\!\big(98,\ \max(55,\ \text{round}(60 + \overline{\text{score}} \times 40))\big).
$$

The ATS score is intentionally clipped between 55 and 98 because a fully zero or perfect score would be misleading to the user.

**Recruiter filters.** Filtering happens before scoring and is plain comparison logic, but it is listed here for completeness. A candidate is included only if both of the following hold:

$$
\text{cand\_exp} \;\geq\; \text{min\_exp},
\qquad
\text{cand\_salary} \;\leq\; \text{max\_salary}\ \ (\text{when max\_salary} > 0).
$$
